Is tip pooling legal? Rules for owners and staff (2026)

A plain-language guide to mandatory tip pools, kitchen staff eligibility under the 2020 FLSA amendments, manager exclusion rules, and credit card fee deductions.

Key Takeaways (At a Glance)

  • • Federal baseline: Mandatory tip pools are fully legal under 29 U.S.C. § 203(m), provided the employer retains zero percent of pool funds for operational overhead or credit margins.
  • • Back-of-House (BOH) inclusion: Line cooks, prep staff, and dishwashers may participate in employer-mandated tip pools only if the operator pays all participating staff the full statutory minimum wage and takes zero tip credit.
  • • Strict manager prohibition: Any supervisor, shift leader, or manager possessing statutory authority to hire, fire, schedule, or supervise is explicitly barred from participating under 29 CFR § 531.54(c)(3)(ii).
  • • State preemption: High-protection states (California, Minnesota, Nevada, Montana, Oregon, Washington, and Alaska) prohibit tip credits entirely and mandate strict state-specific pooling procedures.

1. Federal Baseline: What the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) Allows

Under the United States Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), tips are considered the sole legal property of the employees who receive them. For decades, traditional federal labor doctrine strictly limited mandatory tip pools to workers who “customarily and regularly receive tips”—traditionally servers, bussers, food runners, and bartenders.

However, significant revisions enacted under the Consolidated Appropriations Act and finalized by the Department of Labor (DOL) in late 2020 transformed how restaurant operators structure shift tip distribution. The legality of an employer-mandated pool now turns primarily on a single statutory question: Does the employer take a tip credit against its minimum wage obligation?

If an employer pays tipped employees the sub-minimum direct cash wage (the federal cash wage minimum remains $2.13 per hour while claiming the maximum $5.12 tip credit), the pool remains strictly restricted to Front-of-House (FOH) roles. If the employer pays the standard federal minimum wage ($7.25+) or applicable state minimum out-of-pocket, the pool boundary expands dramatically.

2. The 2020 BOH Rule: Can Kitchen Staff Participate?

Yes. Under the 2020 DOL Tip Regulations rule amending 29 CFR § 531.54, back-of-house employees—including cooks, prep cooks, dishwashers, and expediters—may now legally participate in employer-mandated tip pools, provided the restaurant satisfies two non-negotiable statutory tests:

TEST 01 · Direct Wage Test

Zero Tip-Credit Claimed

The employer must pay all participating servers and bartenders the full prevailing regular minimum wage without offsetting any tip credit.

TEST 02 · Custody Test

Zero Employer Retention

100% of accumulated tips must be disbursed to non-exempt staff. Zero administrative skim or reserve balance may be retained.

This model is referred to under federal law as a “nontraditional tip pool.” When executed properly, it allows dining establishments to narrow the long-standing earnings gap between dining room servers and kitchen teams.

3. The Strict Manager & Supervisor Exclusion (§ 203(m)(2)(B))

Federal law sets an unequivocal boundary: Managers and supervisors may never take any portion of an employee tip pool under any circumstances. Even if a shift supervisor helps bus tables during a rush, rings up takeout orders, or works an entire service alone, they cannot receive tip-pool distributions.

warning FLSA Statutory Compliance Notice

Under 29 U.S.C. § 203(m)(2)(B), any employer, manager, or supervisor who retains employee tips—regardless of whether a tip credit is claimed—faces mandatory disgorgement of all retained tips, 100% liquidated damages, and civil monetary penalties up to $1,330 per violation under 29 CFR § 578.3.

How the DOL Defines a “Supervisor”

The Department of Labor uses the Executive Exemption Duties Test set forth in 29 CFR § 541.100. A staff member is disqualified from the tip pool if they satisfy all three conditions:

  1. Their primary duty is the management of the enterprise, department, or recognized subdivision.
  2. They customarily and regularly direct the work of two or more full-time employees (or the equivalent in part-time workers).
  3. They possess the authority to hire or fire employees, or their suggestions/recommendations regarding hiring, firing, advancement, or scheduling are given particular weight.

Sole proprietors, partners, and corporate officers are completely barred from pools under all conditions. Shift leads without scheduling or disciplinary authority may qualify only if their operational duties are non-managerial.

4. State-Specific Preemption: When Local Rules Override FLSA

Federal labor law establishes a compliance floor, not a ceiling. Where state labor statutes offer broader protections to workers, state law strictly supersedes FLSA guidelines.

5. Credit Card Processing Fees & Tip Deductions

When a patron charges a tip on a credit card, the merchant processor levies an interchange fee (typically 1.5% to 3.5%). Under federal FLSA guidance, an employer may deduct the actual transactional percentage charged by the credit card processor before placing the net tip into the pool.

However, the employer cannot deduct more than the actual transactional rate charged, nor can they turn credit card fee administration into a source of revenue. Crucially, multiple states ban this deduction entirely:

  • Prohibited: California, Colorado, Maine, Massachusetts, Montana, and New York forbid employers from taking any credit card processing deductions from gratuities.
  • Permitted (Federal standard): Texas, Florida, Illinois, Ohio, and Georgia permit deduction of the verifiable card fee.

6. Employer Audit Checklist & Violation Penalties

Hospitality operators facing DOL Wage and Hour Division (WHD) audits must maintain meticulous records. Under 29 CFR § 516.28, employers must preserve weekly tip accounting rosters, showing all credits taken and pools disbursed, for at least three calendar years.
